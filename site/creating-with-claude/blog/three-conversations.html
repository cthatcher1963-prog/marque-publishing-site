Three Conversations That Changed How I Use Claude

Not tips. Not techniques. Three moments where I realized I'd been thinking about AI collaboration wrong.

The first was a failure. I'd asked Claude to analyze a cybersecurity incident report and give me a board-ready summary. It produced something polished, professional, and completely useless — the kind of executive summary that says everything and communicates nothing. I almost used it. Then I read it out loud and realized it was missing the one thing that would have made a board member sit up: what this incident meant for our company, specifically.

I'd given Claude the incident report but none of the company context. No industry, no risk posture, no board personality. The AI did exactly what I asked. The problem was what I didn't ask.

The second conversation was an experiment. Instead of giving Claude a task, I gave it a problem and asked it to push back on my assumptions. I'd been framing a chapter around a particular argument, and something felt off but I couldn't identify it. So I described my argument and said, "What am I missing? Be direct."

It identified a logical gap I'd been unconsciously stepping over for two weeks. Not because the AI was smarter — because it didn't have my blind spots. It saw my argument as text, not as something I'd invested emotional energy in defending. That's when I understood that AI's best role isn't answering your questions. It's challenging your thinking.

The third conversation wasn't one conversation. It was the moment I started treating AI sessions as ongoing work rather than one-off transactions. Loading context from previous sessions. Building on earlier decisions. Referring back to analyses we'd done together. The output quality jumped dramatically because the AI finally had enough context to do its real work.

Three moments. Each one shifted something fundamental in how I approached the tool. The details of what I changed — and the methodology that grew out of these shifts — are the spine of the book. But the moments themselves came from getting things wrong enough to notice.

Most people's breakthrough with AI won't come from a better prompt. It'll come from a conversation that surprises them.
