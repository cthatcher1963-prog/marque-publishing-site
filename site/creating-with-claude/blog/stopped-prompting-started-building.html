The Moment I Stopped Prompting and Started Building

I spent my first three months with Claude asking it questions.

Good questions, mostly. How does this API work? Can you explain this concept? Write me a summary of that article. I was using the most powerful creative tool of my generation as a slightly faster search engine.

The shift didn't happen because I read a tutorial or discovered a secret prompt format. It happened because I got frustrated. I'd been staring at a problem — a publishing calculator I wanted to build for other authors — and I realized I'd been "researching" it for two weeks. Asking Claude to explain printing costs. Asking about KDP royalty structures. Asking about margin calculations.

At some point I stopped asking and said: "Let's build this."

That's the moment. Not the first time you type something into an AI. The first time you say "let's make this thing" and mean it. The gap between those two moments is where most people get stuck — permanently.

When you're asking questions, the AI is in charge. It decides what to show you. When you're building something, you're in charge. The AI becomes your tool, your collaborator, your sparring partner. The power dynamic flips, and everything about the experience changes.

I built that calculator in a weekend. Not because I'm a developer — I'm not, or at least I wasn't. I built it because I finally stopped preparing to build and started building. The calculator works. It does real math with real KDP pricing from 2026. It's on the site right now, free to use.

But here's what nobody tells you about that moment: it doesn't just change what you can do with AI. It changes how you see problems. Once you've built one thing, your brain starts seeing build opportunities everywhere. That email system you hate. That spreadsheet workflow that wastes an hour a week. That tool you wish existed for your job. Suddenly, "somebody should build that" becomes "I could build that this weekend."

The prompting-to-building shift isn't a technique. It's a perspective change. And it's the single most important transition that separates people who think AI is "kind of useful" from people whose careers are being reshaped by it.

I wrote a whole book about what happens after that moment — the methodology, the projects, the mistakes that taught me the most. But the book can't give you the moment itself. That part you have to reach on your own. Or, if you're like me, you have to get frustrated enough that research stops feeling like progress.

What are you researching right now that you should be building?
