Cybersecurity July 2026

The Patch Your Company Never Approved

On Friday, WordPress shipped an emergency fix for a flaw researchers named wp2shell — a chain of two bugs that lets an anonymous web request take full control of a site running WordPress 6.9 or 7.0. No login, no plugins, just a default installation answering traffic on the open internet.

Then the maintainers did something most corporate change processes would never allow: they began force-pushing the fix to affected sites through WordPress's automatic update system. No ticket. No maintenance window. Cloudflare moved the same afternoon, deploying protective firewall rules for sites behind its service — by its own account, at 17:03 UTC.

If your web properties had automatic updates enabled, there's a decent chance you were patched before anyone on your team read the advisory. If someone turned auto-updates off — usually in the name of change control — you're on the other list. More on that in a minute.

Why This Reaches Your Company

W3Techs measures WordPress at roughly 42% of all websites. Your core business may run on something else entirely, but some corner of your company almost certainly touches WordPress: the marketing site, the product blog, the campaign microsite an agency spun up two years ago that nobody decommissioned.

In my experience, the corporate website is the asset everyone sees and nobody owns. Marketing pays for it. An agency hosts it. IT finds out it exists when it breaks — or when it gets breached. It rarely appears in the asset inventory, and it almost never appears in the audit.

The facts, in plain terms. Adam Kues, a researcher at Assetnote — the attack surface management arm of Searchlight Cyber — found the flaw and reported it through WordPress's HackerOne bug bounty program. Searchlight withheld the technical details to buy site owners time and published a checker at wp2shell.com instead. Affected versions: 6.9.0 through 6.9.4 and 7.0.0 through 7.0.1. Fixed versions: 6.9.5 and 7.0.2, released July 17. GitHub's security advisory classifies the issue as critical; the two bugs are tracked as CVE-2026-63030 and CVE-2026-60137.

Here's the part worth reading twice. On Friday, Rapid7 assessed that a public exploit was "highly likely" to appear quickly — their reasoning being that WordPress is open source, the patch is public, and AI models have gotten good at reading code changes and working backward to the bug. Beazley Security made the same call: expect threat actors to reverse-engineer the patch and go after WordPress installations at scale. By Saturday, security outlets were reporting that working proof-of-concept code was already public. The prediction took about a day to come true. As of this writing, no confirmed compromises have been publicly reported — the race between patching and exploitation is happening right now, this weekend.

The Control Nobody Chose

Sit with the forced-update decision for a moment, because it cuts against everything enterprise IT is taught.

WordPress's maintainers stated they are forcing the update onto affected installations that have automatic updates enabled. They decided speed mattered more than anyone's change process, and for a flaw like this one, they were right. Most of the sites protected this weekend were protected by a default setting their owners never thought about.

But notice the qualifier: installations with automatic updates enabled. Plenty of organizations disable auto-updates on web properties — sometimes for stability, sometimes because a change policy written for internal systems got applied to the public website. Those sites got nothing on Friday. They are waiting on a human who may not know they exist.

And even where auto-update is on, don't assume it worked. Rapid7's guidance is to verify that every internet-facing WordPress site actually upgraded. Hadrian, a security firm that analyzed the patch, was blunter: auto-updates can stall quietly on file-permission or disk problems, so treat "core is on auto-update" as something to verify, not a control to assume.

Three Questions, Applied

This is a clean test of the Three Questions framework from Cyber Risk Is Business Risk.

What do we have? How many WordPress sites carry your brand right now? Count the agency-run sites and the staging copies. Hadrian's advisory singles out "the staging and campaign sites everyone forgets" — because those are the ones that miss the update.

What are we doing to protect it? For each site: what version is it running, verified by an actual check rather than an assumption? Is auto-update on? Who owns the answer — an employee, or an agency contact who left last year?

What happens if we fail? Full control of the site means full control of what your customers see, plus access to whatever the site's database holds — form fills, newsletter signups, sometimes commerce data. Hadrian notes that many WordPress sites sit on shared hosting, where one compromised instance is rarely the end of the blast radius. And the cleanup bill is the small cost. Your homepage quietly serving malware to customers is the large one.

The Audit Scope Problem

Your SOC 2 report almost certainly says nothing about the marketing site. It sits outside the audit boundary, so the auditor never looks at it. Meanwhile it is the most public thing you own, it collects prospect data all day, and until Friday it may have been one anonymous request away from takeover.

Compliance asks whether the systems in scope are patched. Security asks whether everything with your name on it is. wp2shell is the cleanest example of that gap I've seen this year.

What to Ask Your CISO This Week

Three asks, all answerable by Friday.

First: "Give me the list — every WordPress site we own or pay someone to run, including staging and agency-hosted." If producing the list takes more than a few days, that's finding number one, and it has nothing to do with WordPress.

Second: "Confirm each one is on 6.9.5, 7.0.2, or later — verified, not assumed." The wp2shell.com checker exists precisely so you don't have to take auto-update on faith.

Third: "Where have we disabled automatic updates on public-facing sites, and who accepted that risk?" There may be good answers. There should at least be an owner.

The sites that stayed safe this weekend were mostly saved by a default setting. The ones that get hit in the coming weeks will mostly be the ones nobody remembered they owned. Which list your company lands on comes down to inventory — and that question is answerable before the exploit finishes making the rounds.
