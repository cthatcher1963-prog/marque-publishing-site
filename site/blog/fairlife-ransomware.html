When Ransomware Stops the Milk

On July 16, Coca-Cola filed an 8-K with the SEC disclosing that fairlife, its ultra-filtered dairy subsidiary, was hit by ransomware. The attackers reached production-related systems. Every fairlife production operation in the United States is suspended.

Not a data breach announcement. A dairy that can't make milk.

I've sat with executive teams during ransomware recoveries. The conversation everyone remembers afterward is the operations chief telling the CEO, "I don't know when we can ship product again." Stolen data has a playbook — lawyers, notification letters, credit monitoring. A stopped production line has a meter running, and nobody in the room can say what the final number will be.

That conversation is happening at fairlife right now.

What the filing says — and what it doesn't

Coca-Cola's disclosure is short and careful. Fairlife identified unauthorized third-party access to a portion of its systems, "including its production-related systems, in connection with a ransomware event." The company activated its incident response and business continuity protocols and brought in outside cybersecurity experts. Law enforcement has been notified. Product quality and safety are not affected. U.S. production is suspended; Canadian operations are running.

What the filing doesn't say matters just as much. No attacker is named, and as of this writing no ransomware group has claimed the attack. There is no timeline for restoring production. Coca-Cola states plainly that "the full scope, nature and impacts of the incident are not yet known."

Sit with the Canada detail for a moment. Coca-Cola hasn't said why Canadian plants were spared. But when one region keeps producing while another goes dark, the boundary between those networks did its job. Network segmentation is unglamorous budget-line work — right up until the day it's the reason half your operation still runs.

Downtime has a price tag

Fairlife is not a rounding error inside Coca-Cola. CEO James Quincey told investors the brand grew from $10 million in retail sales in 2014 to nearly $4 billion in 2024. Coca-Cola paid $979 million in early 2020 to buy out its joint-venture partner and take full ownership.

Every day those plants sit idle, that engine is off. Dairy adds a complication most industries never face: the raw material is perishable and the supply doesn't pause. Milk comes from cows, and cows don't have a maintenance window.

For a sense of what production downtime costs at this scale, look at United Natural Foods. When the grocery distributor was hit last June, core systems took roughly three weeks to restore, and UNFI told investors the incident would reduce net sales by an estimated $350 to $400 million. Shoppers saw the abstract made concrete: thin shelves at Whole Foods stores across the country.

The fairlife attackers created a nine-figure risk without touching a single customer record. They stopped the plant.

The materiality clock

Here's the part your board should study. Coca-Cola filed this under Item 8.01 of Form 8-K — "Other Events" — rather than Item 1.05, the SEC's mandatory cybersecurity incident disclosure. The filing explains why: the company "has not yet determined whether the incident is reasonably likely to materially affect" it.

That determination carries a deadline. Under the SEC's cyber disclosure rules, once a company concludes an incident is material, it has four business days to tell the public. Somewhere in Atlanta, executives and counsel are running a live materiality analysis on an incident whose full scope they admit they don't know yet.

If this were your company, who makes that call, and on what information? Would the first run-through be the real thing, or has someone rehearsed it?

In my experience, disclosure gets treated as a compliance chore that starts after the technical team finishes. Watch how the well-run companies do it. Coca-Cola announced the production suspension and filed with precise, lawyered language while the investigation was still young. That speed doesn't happen by accident. Someone practiced before the incident.

What to ask your CISO this week

Three questions, and the last one belongs to your CFO too.

"If ransomware reached our production systems tonight, how long until we ship again?" Ask for the number from the last actual test, not the recovery time objective in the binder. If production and plant-floor systems have never been part of a recovery exercise, you just found this quarter's priority.

"Where are the walls?" Fairlife's Canadian plants kept running while U.S. operations stopped. Ask what separates your sites from corporate IT and from each other. If the honest answer is "not much," one phishing email in accounting can idle every location you operate.

"What does one day of stopped operations cost us, by site?" If nobody can produce that number within an hour, your security budget is being set on instinct. UNFI's number turned out to be $350 million or more in lost sales. Learn yours before an attacker teaches it to you.

Coca-Cola will absorb this. It has the scale, it had a boundary that held, and its disclosure operation was clearly rehearsed. Now look at your own company and ask which of those three you could claim this morning. If the answer is none, you have your next board agenda.
